# Analog Cutout Collage

## Aesthetic Thesis
The screen behaves like a studio desk mid-assembly: fragments of paper snipped
with scissors, layered at deliberate angles, pinned and taped into a composition
that feels found rather than designed. In three seconds the eye catches the
irregular edges, the soft shadow beneath each cutout, and the quiet evidence of
a hand that cut, placed, and reconsidered.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Warm, resourceful, slightly restless — the mood of a Dada collage table or a
sketchbook spread. Reach for it on cultural essays, personal narratives, mood
boards, and anything that benefits from tactile, fragmentary storytelling. It
fights clinical precision and corporate calm; the handmade irregularity that
reads as authentic here would read as messy in a data readout.

## Color Temperament
Live inside a warm paper world: cream, manila, and aged-stock tones as ground,
with cutout fragments pulled from a broader but still earthy palette — a
rust-red scrap, a faded indigo snippet, a mustard tear. One saturated cutout may
serve as a focal anchor, but it earns that role by being the exception. Shadows
are warm and soft, reading as paper depth rather than UI elevation. Why: the
warmth of paper and the restraint of a found-material palette is what sells the
illusion; a cool, saturated, or gradient-rich palette exposes the screen and
breaks the desk. Refuse pure white, gradient blends, and any color that looks
mixed rather than torn from something.

## Typographic Voice
Pair a handwritten or marker-scrawled annotation voice with a quiet, typeset
body voice that reads as clipped from a book or journal. The handwritten voice
is the margin note, arrow label, circled emphasis — personal, immediate,
slightly uneven. The typeset voice is the fragment: a headline torn
from a newspaper, a paragraph clipped from a pamphlet, set with the authority of
print but the irregularity of a cut edge. Hierarchy emerges from which fragment
sits on top, which is largest, and where the eye lands first in the pile. Why:
collage lives on the tension between printed authority and hand-marked urgency;
a single comfortable voice would read as a layout, not a collection of pieces.

## Spatial Temperament
Medium-high density with deliberate overlap and refusal to snap. Fragments drift
at angles, sit partially off-frame, and cover one another — the composition
reads as assembled, not gridded. A loose grid may guide placement, but its
evidence must never be visible. The invariant is the
relationship of overlap: at least two fragments must intersect, and the topmost
piece must cast a visible shadow onto what lies beneath. Where things sit and
how many there are is free; that they read as cut paper in a pile is not.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface is paper — fibrous, slightly textured, warm to the touch. Depth
comes from real paper-stacking shadows: each cutout sits above the layer beneath
it, with a soft, short shadow that reads as a thin sheet catching desk light,
not as a floating card. Tape strips and pin dots are the fasteners, visible and
functional. The air is intimate and studio-lit; nothing glows or backlights.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring maker's marks: irregular scissor-cut edges on every shape (never a
clean vector curve), soft paper-stacking shadows beneath each fragment,
translucent tape strips crossing joints or corners, pin-prick dots at corners,
hand-scrawled arrows and annotations pointing at things, and at least one
fragment that bleeds off the edge of the frame. These are evidence of cutting
and pinning, not decoration. Use with intent: tape holds a layer down, a pin
secures a pivot, an annotation explains a connection.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement should feel like a hand placing a scrap: a quick, slightly uneven
settle, as if a fragment was set down and nudged once.
Favor entrances that read as physical placement — a small rotation as it lands,
a slight overshoot before it rests — never smooth glides or fades. Interaction
is blunt: a click feels like pressing a pin through paper, a hover like tilting
the desk to see what is underneath. Why: the motion must agree with the handmade
material logic; buttery digital easing contradicts the scissor-and-tape premise
and reminds the viewer they are looking at a screen.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms
Identity Invariants:
- Warm paper-textured ground, never pure white or smooth
- Irregular scissor-cut edges on every shape — no clean vector curves
- Overlapping fragments with visible paper-stacking shadows
- Handwritten annotation voice present somewhere on each slide
- At least one tape strip or pin fastener per composition
- At least one fragment bleeding off the frame edge

Freedoms:
- Number of fragments and their sizes
- Exact paper tones and accent hues
- Angles of rotation and overlap relationships
- Which content lives in which fragment
- Whether the handwritten voice annotates, labels, or emphasizes
- Which slides earn a tape strip, a pin, or both

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies the moment it gets smooth: perfectly aligned edges, gradient blends,
soft rounded corners, pure white backgrounds, or buttery ease-ins all strip the
paper-cut illusion. Too many saturated colors, pixel-perfect grids, or any depth
that reads as UI elevation rather than paper stacking kills the handmade
register. If the content wants the feeling of a printed zine — ink on paper,
registration drift, two-color constraint — rather than cut-and-paste collage,
switch to Riso Print Zine.
