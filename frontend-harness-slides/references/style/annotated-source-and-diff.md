# Annotated Source & Diff

## Aesthetic Thesis
A high-fidelity code diff or source annotator that shows a before-and-after
transformation in a single view, making the value of a change impossible to miss.
The point is proof: you can see exactly what changed and why it is better.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Reviewable, precise, and transformation-focused — the register of a careful code
review. Reach for it on before/after transformations, diff explanations, and text
or code annotation, where showing the change side by side is the argument. As a
reading-first style it carries real detail, but it fights abstract vision slides;
without a concrete before and after, there is nothing to diff.

## Color Temperament
Keep a clean, light ground and use soft tinted highlight fields to mark change — a
gentle removal tone and a gentle addition tone — with crisp markers for
annotations. Color encodes change, never decoration. Why: the whole point is
reading what was removed versus added at a glance, so the removal/addition tones
must be the only strong color; a busy palette would drown the diff.

## Typographic Voice
Pair a sharp, clean monospaced character for the source or diffed text with a
clean readable voice for the annotations, exactly as a real reviewer mixes code
and commentary. Keep the source aligned and the annotations legible. Why: the
credibility of a diff rests on the source looking like real code and the notes
reading clearly; without the monospaced/annotation split it stops looking like a
review.

## Spatial Temperament
It wants a comparison-in-one-view sensibility: a before and an after that read as
clearly related — stacked, paired, or annotated in place — so the transformation
is obvious. Whether it is a before/after diff or an annotated source with
side notes is free; the invariant is a legible, related before-and-after with the
change marked.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface feels like a clean reviewer: light panels, tinted change fields, crisp
code blocks, tidy annotation markers. Depth is minimal — separation from tints and
panels, not shadow. The atmosphere is precise, focused, and proof-oriented.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring identity marks are review vocabulary: removal and addition
highlights, strike-throughs, annotation markers or callouts, and a clear
before/after pairing. Use them to carry the real transformation — real removals,
real additions, real reasons. The marked before-and-after grammar is the
signature; an unmarked single block breaks it.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement is transformation: the "before" resolves into the "after," changed
regions highlight, an annotation points to what changed. Interaction can toggle
before/after or reveal an annotation. Keep motion focused on the change. Why: the
animation is proving the transformation, so motion unrelated to the diff would
waste the view.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms
Keep constant to stay this style: a clean light ground with soft removal/addition
change tones, a sharp monospaced source voice paired with a readable annotation
voice, a legible related before-and-after with change marked, minimal reviewer
depth, and change-focused motion. Free to vary on every slide: stacked diff versus
annotated source, what is being transformed, and how the change is revealed.

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies when the change gets lost: a dark or busy background, decorative color
competing with the change tones, an expressive typeface with no monospaced source,
a single unmarked block with no before/after, or motion unrelated to the diff. If
the content is an abstract vision with no concrete change, there is nothing to
annotate — use a keynote or memo style instead. If the content is a ticket-framed change with status and labeled sections rather than a pure diff view, switch to Maintainer Issue Brief. If it needs a formal reasoning chain explaining why the change was made instead of just showing what changed, move to Decision Record.
