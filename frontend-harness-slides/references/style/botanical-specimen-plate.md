# Botanical Specimen Plate

## Aesthetic Thesis
The screen behaves like a pressed herbarium sheet opened under a north-facing
window: a single specimen, rendered with the patient precision of 19th-century
scientific illustration, held still on aged paper so the eye can trace every vein
and contour without hurry. The three-second impression is quiet wonder — the
feeling of encountering something rare and carefully observed.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Scholarly, reverent, unhurried — the mood of a naturalist's study rather than a
marketplace. Reach for it on taxonomy, classification, species catalogs,
systematic comparisons, and any content that benefits from the authority of
pre-photographic observation. It fights urgency, saturation, and dashboard
density; the calm that reads as contemplative here would read as lifeless in a
launch or a pitch.

## Color Temperament
The ground is aged paper — a warm bone or cream that has absorbed a century of
ambient light, never the clinical flatness of pure white. Linework lives in the
realm of iron-gall ink and sepia: deep, warm, slightly uneven in tone, as if
hand-applied. Color accents are drawn exclusively from a muted naturalist's
palette — desaturated botanical greens, earth ochres, the faintest rose of a
dried petal — and used with extreme scarcity. No saturation anywhere, ever.
Why: the power of this style comes from the illusion of physical age and
patient craft; a single vivid hue reads as a modern intrusion and shatters the
herbarium spell. Refuse pure blacks, electric blues, and anything that glows.

## Typographic Voice
Body text carries the character of oldstyle serif letterforms — warm, slightly
eccentric at the joints, readable at length, with the quiet authority of a
book set before photography existed. Latin binomials and taxonomic terms wear
a true italic that feels hand-angled rather than mechanically sloped. Plate
labels, figure numbers, and captions speak in small capitals — compact,
even-colored, archival in feel. Hierarchy is gentle: the specimen image leads
by scale and centrality, not by shouting; captions and labels whisper
supporting information at a fraction of the body size. Why: the typography
must feel contemporaneous with the engraving tradition it inhabits; a modern
geometric or a high-contrast display would read as a different century
entirely.

## Spatial Temperament
The composition is symmetrical and centered, as if the specimen were mounted
under glass with a label pinned beneath. Density is low — one focal organism
or figure per plate, surrounded by generous margins of aged paper. The grid
feels invisible but precise, like the faint layout lines a 19th-century
engraver would have scored in pencil and then erased. The invariant is the
relationship of specimen to label: the image dominates the upper two-thirds,
the taxonomic caption and plate number anchor the lower third. Everything
else — exact scale, margin width, whether a plate number sits flush left or
centered — is free.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface is paper: fibrous, warm, slightly uneven in tone, carrying the
subtle texture of something that has been handled and stored. Depth comes not
from shadows or elevation but from the layering of ink linework — fine
hatching, cross-hatching, stippling — that builds form the way an engraver
builds it, one line at a time. The atmosphere is still, herbarium-calm;
nothing moves, nothing glows, nothing competes for attention.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring identity marks are the furniture of a scientific plate: a plate
number set in small capitals, a taxonomic caption with the Latin binomial in
italic, a collector's attribution or locality note in the smallest voice.
These are not decoration — they are the apparatus of observation, the
scholarly apparatus that tells the viewer this specimen was found, recorded,
and classified. Use them with intent: every label must carry real information
about what is on the plate, not fill empty space.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement is nearly imperceptible — the slow settling of dust, the soft turn of
a page, a gentle fade-in that feels like the specimen coming into focus under
a lens. Nothing bounces, nothing snaps, nothing pulses. Interaction, if it
exists at all, is the quiet act of leaning in: hovering to reveal a detail,
tapping to magnify a structure. Why: the herbarium is a place of stillness;
any motion that calls attention to itself contradicts the centuries-of-rest
feeling the whole style cultivates.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms

Identity Invariants:
- Aged-paper warm ground, never pure white or cool gray
- Fine ink linework in sepia or iron-gall character, never vector-crisp
- Muted, desaturated natural palette with zero saturation
- Taxonomic label furniture: plate number, binomial caption, attribution
- Symmetrical specimen-centered composition
- Oldstyle serif body with true italic for Latin names; small caps for labels
- Herbarium stillness — motion is barely there

Freedoms:
- Which organism or specimen is depicted
- Exact scale of the specimen within its plate
- Margin widths and label placement details
- Choice of muted accent hue (green, ochre, rose) per plate
- Whether a plate includes a single specimen or a comparative series
- The specific engraving technique suggested (hatching, stippling, outline)

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies the moment anything feels digital or urgent: saturated colors, pure
white backgrounds, glossy surfaces, hard vector edges, drop shadows, fast
motion, dashboard information density, multiple competing elements, or a
modern sans-serif. If the content calls for contemporary organic warmth,
living green energy, or a mid-century modernist take on nature rather than
19th-century scientific illustration, switch to Mid-Century Grove instead. If
the content needs light-background technical blueprint precision with
drafting-table calm rather than herbarium plate reverence, switch to Cyanotype
Drafting Table.
