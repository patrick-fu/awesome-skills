# Cassette-Era Packaging

## Aesthetic Thesis
The screen behaves like a well-preserved cassette J-card pulled from a 1970s
Japanese audio-products catalogue: warm cream paper, a diagonal rainbow ribbon
slicing across it, dense spec-sheet furniture, and condensed bold type that
shouts with the confidence of a product that knows exactly what it is. The
three-second impression is "this is a thing, and it has specs."

## Emotional Register & Fit
Nostalgic, precise, and quietly enthusiastic — the mood of finding a beloved
artifact in a used record shop. Reach for it on product launches with retro-tech
nostalgia, indie tool releases, or any deck where the product itself feels like
a physical object worth cataloguing. It fights serious enterprise, organic
naturalism, and corporate sobriety; the cassette-package warmth reads as
flippant in a boardroom.

## Color Temperament
The world is warm cream paper — a slightly darkened cream that reads as aged
print stock. All structural ink is a deep warm brown rather than pure black, so
every line carries print-on-paper warmth. The only color intrusion is a
diagonal rainbow ribbon: five stacked solid-color bars sweeping across the
composition at a fixed diagonal angle. This ribbon is the system's sole
chromatic event and must be used sparingly — it anchors a cover or closing
spread, not every routine slide. Why: the ribbon's power comes from scarcity against the
monochrome cream-and-ink field; if color leaks into body elements, the
cassette-package illusion collapses into generic rainbow branding.

## Typographic Voice
Display type is a condensed, heavy-weight industrial sans — tall, narrow, and
loud, like the product name stamped across a cassette inlay. It carries
negative letter-spacing to compress the forms and amplify catalogue-cover
density. Body text is a clean, neutral humanist sans at regular weight.
Tabular and spec data uses a monospaced voice that reads as technical
specification rather than editorial prose. The hierarchy drops
sharply: display shouts, body whispers, mono ticks. Why: the face-to-face
contrast — industrial display next to clean body next to mechanical data — is
what makes the page feel like a curated product sheet; one voice throughout
erases the cassette-package conceit.

## Spatial Temperament
Medium-high density, like a packed catalogue page where every region does
concurrent work. A topbar with title and meta-label sits above a body grid of
product cards, spec tables, or equalizer bar charts. Cover and manifesto
spreads break into free composition — petals, ribbons, hero numerals layered
across the canvas. The invariant is the frame inset: a consistent margin from
the slide edges creating the printed-page feeling, with a page number anchored
at the bottom-right. What fills the frame is free.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface is warm cream paper with a faint halftone-dot grain — the texture
of offset print on coated stock, present at low opacity so it grounds every
flat color block without competing. Depth comes from hard offset shadows (a
solid ink-colored drop with zero blur, reserved for quote callouts that earn
elevation), ink borders around cards, and color-block layering — never soft
blurred shadows. The atmosphere is hand-curated and slightly sun-faded.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring identity marks are: a diagonal multi-color ribbon band sweeping
behind hero content; a cluster of overlapping perfect-circle blobs in the
primary palette as a corner brand-mark; a starburst seal filled with ink
carrying a short glyph; a red rectangular stamp with cream text for status
badges; and square spec checkboxes marked with a cream multiplication sign —
never a checkmark — the JIS-style feature checklist. Each is a maker's mark,
deployed with intent and never as decoration.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement is brisk and mechanical, like a cassette tape engaging — quick opacity
fades between slides, no easing flourishes, no playful bounces. If elements
reveal, they snap in rather than drift. Interaction, if present, is functional
and tabular — selecting a spec row, toggling a checkbox — never atmospheric.
Why: the cassette-package aesthetic is about the object and its specifications,
not performance; slow cinematic motion contradicts the no-nonsense product-sheet
register.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms

Identity Invariants:
- Warm cream paper ground with faint halftone-dot texture
- Deep warm-brown ink for all structural lines and text (never pure black)
- One diagonal rainbow ribbon band as the sole chromatic accent, used sparingly
- Condensed heavy-weight display with negative tracking
- Clean neutral body voice with a monospaced voice for all spec and tabular data
- Square spec checkboxes with multiplication-sign marks (not checkmarks)
- Consistent frame inset with bottom-right page number
- Hard offset shadows only — no soft blur anywhere

Freedoms:
- Which slides earn the ribbon treatment and petal cluster placement
- Seal and stamp usage frequency
- Product card count and grid arrangement
- Equalizer bar column count and per-column color assignment
- Ledger row data and chip color selection
- Whether Japanese character accents appear
- Cover composition and free-layer layout

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies when it gets corporate: pure white backgrounds, black ink, soft drop
shadows, rounded corners, a friendly rounded display face, or multiple accent
colors scattered across body elements. It also breaks on organic naturalism —
soft gradients, hand-drawn illustrations, botanical warmth — and on serious
enterprise contexts where retro-tech nostalgia reads as unprofessional. If the
content wants OS-level desktop nostalgia instead of cassette-package nostalgia,
switch to Retro Windows. If it needs corporate sobriety, move to Research Memo.
If it calls for arcade game retro with pixel sprites and HUD meters rather than
cassette product catalogue warmth, switch to Arcade Boss Fight.
