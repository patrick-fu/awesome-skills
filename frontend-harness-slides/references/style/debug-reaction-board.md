# Debug Reaction Board

## Aesthetic Thesis
A developer's diagnostic board — part status panel, part kanban — where a system
is visibly self-checking, tagging risks, and making its uncertainty explicit
before it acts. The point is that caution and diagnosis are shown, not hidden.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Developer-native, diagnostic, and candid — the register of a good status readout.
Reach for it on system diagnostics, risk assessment, readiness checks, and
remediation, where surfacing what is safe, uncertain, or blocked is the message.
It works live and reads afterward, but it fights non-technical audiences and
emotional stories; without checks and states to show, the board is empty.

## Color Temperament
Set a deep, dark IDE-like ground so status colors read like signals on a
terminal, using a small, strict traffic-light vocabulary — a healthy tone, an
attention tone, a high-risk tone — to encode state. The colors are status, never
decoration. Why: the dark developer ground plus disciplined status color is what
makes risk legible at a glance; decorative color would destroy the diagnostic
clarity.

## Typographic Voice
Favor a monospaced, terminal-like character throughout, because the developer
feel and the alignment of statuses and tags depend on it. Keep labels crisp and
machine-like. Why: the credibility of a diagnostic board rests on looking like a
real tool; a soft or proportional voice would undercut the terminal atmosphere.

## Spatial Temperament
It wants a legible set of check items or states, each carrying a clear status, so
the composition reads as a system reporting on itself. Whether it is a vertical
self-check list, risk columns (safe / needs-verification / blocked), or another
diagnostic arrangement is free; the invariant is discrete items each showing an
explicit, color-coded state.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface feels like a dark developer tool: matte slate panels, glowing status
badges, terminal blocks, crisp edges. Depth comes from panel layering and badge
glow, not realistic light. The atmosphere is focused, technical, and honest about
risk.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring identity marks are diagnostic vocabulary: check or issue cards,
color-coded status badges (clear, uncertain, high-risk), and columns or lists
that sort items by state. Use them to carry real checks and real risk levels. The
explicit-status-per-item grammar is the signature; unlabeled cards with no state
break it.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement is status change: a check resolves and its badge flips state, a card
moves between risk columns, a diagnostic result reveals. Interaction can expand a
card to show detail or step through the self-check. Keep motion crisp and
state-driven. Why: the animation is narrating the system checking itself, so
decorative motion unrelated to status would blur the diagnosis.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms
Keep constant to stay this style: a deep dark developer ground, a strict status
color vocabulary, a monospaced terminal voice, discrete items each with an
explicit color-coded state, panel-and-badge depth, and state-driven motion. Free
to vary on every slide: list versus kanban versus other diagnostic arrangement,
which checks and risks appear, and how status changes are revealed.

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies when it stops looking like a tool: a light or warm background, decorative
non-status color, a soft proportional typeface, generic cards with no states, or
motion unrelated to status. If the audience is non-technical or the content is a
story or claim, the diagnostic frame alienates them — use a warmer or simpler
style instead.
