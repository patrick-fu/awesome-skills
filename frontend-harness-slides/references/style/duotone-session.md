# Duotone Session

## Aesthetic Thesis
The screen behaves like a Blue Note LP sleeve: a photograph bathed in a single
ink tint, enormous gothic type stacked asymmetrically around it, the whole thing
humming with the rhythm of a session date. In three seconds the eye lands on the
photo, feels the scale jump, and registers the cool discipline of a recording
that knows exactly what it is.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Cool, disciplined, and workmanlike — the mood of a late-night studio session
where craft speaks louder than ego. Reach for it on product launches with a
musical or cultural angle, artist or team spotlights, and any deck that wants
editorial grit without fashion gloss. It fights corporate polish, playful
illustrations, and data-heavy readouts; the flat-ink toughness that reads as
authentic here would feel cold in a boardroom.

## Color Temperament
Live in a rich, warm black — never pure screen black, always a hair toward
charcoal so it feels like ink on paper rather than a void. Over a black-and-white
photograph lay a single flat spot-ink tint: cobalt, ember, or teal in feeling,
applied as a uniform wash, never a gradient. A second flat field is allowed as a
rare structural move for credits or a label, but never more. Accent is scarcity:
the spot ink earns its authority by touching only the photo. Why: the duotone is
the identity; a third color or a full-color photograph turns this into a
magazine layout and kills the session mood. Refuse gradients, glow, and any
color carrying body text.

## Typographic Voice
Two voices share the stage. The dominant is a workmanlike gothic sans — sturdy,
condensed, unadorned, the spirit of trade posters and newspaper headlines —
pushed to a scale so large it becomes image rather than text. The second voice,
used sparingly for credits or a single line of emphasis, is a high-contrast
serif that cuts through the gothic weight like a musician's signature. The scale
gap between them is radical: the title dwarfs the credit line, and
that jump is the point. Why: gothic-as-image anchors the session-poster
tradition; shrinking the title to a polite header collapses the whole identity.

## Spatial Temperament
The photo leads. It occupies the majority of the canvas — often bleeding to one
or more edges — and type wraps around it asymmetrically, like instruments in a
combo rather than a centered orchestra. The composition feels stacked and
rhythmic: a massive title block anchored to a corner or edge, credits tucked
into a band, negative space used as a rest in the score. The invariant is the
photo's dominance and the asymmetric musical stacking; exact placement, which
edge bleeds, and how the type wraps are all free.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface is flat ink on heavy stock. No gloss, sheen, or simulated depth
from shadows or glass. Texture, if any, comes from the photo grain and flat ink
solidity — never noise overlays or paper textures. The atmosphere is a dim
studio after hours: cool, focused, a little smoky, and entirely without pretense.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring marks are the duotone tint, the enormous condensed gothic title
block at a corner or edge, a thin credit band holding tiny type, and the
occasional flat-ink field as a structural shelf. These are the session's
instrumentation, not decorations. Use each with intent and never stack all four
on one slide.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement has a rhythmic, percussive feel: a sharp type block snapping into
place, a photo tint locking on, a credit line sliding in like a bass line
settling. Easing is decisive rather than soft — things arrive with intent and
stop clean. Never floaty, never bouncy, never a slow dissolve that blurs the
edge of the flat ink. Interaction is tactile: a hard tap, a clean panel shift, a
photo swap that feels like changing a record. Why: built on flat fields and hard
edges, motion that blurs or glows contradicts the ink-on-paper material.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms
Identity Invariants:
- Rich warm black ground, never pure screen black
- One flat spot-ink tint as a duotone over a black-and-white photo
- Photo-first composition — the image dominates the canvas
- Gothic sans used as image-scale display type
- Radical scale contrast between title and supporting text
- Asymmetric musical stacking of elements
- Flat-ink material — no gradients, glow, or simulated depth

Freedoms:
- The specific spot-ink hue (cobalt, ember, teal, or another single flat color)
- Which edges the photo bleeds to and its exact crop
- Placement of the gothic title block (corner, edge, wrapping the photo)
- Whether a second flat-ink field appears and where
- The exact scale ratio between title and credits
- Which slide carries a serif accent and which stays purely gothic
- Content, tone, and subject — product, people, and culture all fit

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies when the photo goes full color — the duotone tint is the session's
signature, and without it you are looking at a magazine spread. It also dies
when scale gets timid: shrink the gothic title to a comfortable header and the
whole thing collapses into a generic dark slide. Other killers: extra accent
colors, gradients, glow, centered layouts, playful illustration, and body text in
the spot ink. For full-color photos or glossy fashion energy, switch to Magazine
Masthead.
