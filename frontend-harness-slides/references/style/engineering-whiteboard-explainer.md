# Engineering Whiteboard Explainer

Also known as `工程讲解白板`.

## Aesthetic Thesis
A clean white engineering canvas where a technical idea is explained step by
step: structured cards, routed arrows, annotated states, and sticker-like emoji
objects turn an abstract system into something the audience can follow beat by
beat. It should feel like a clear engineer is teaching at a prepared white
canvas, not like a messy workshop wall or a decorative sketch.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Clear, engineering-led, and explanatory - the register of a sharp technical
walkthrough. Reach for it on architecture talks, workflow methods, system
mechanisms, developer education, and process stories where each click should
advance the argument. It works when the deck needs both human warmth and
engineering precision. It fights formal reports, pure issue trackers, and
artistic illustration decks; without a mechanism to explain, the canvas becomes
empty decoration.

## Color Temperament
Start from a true white canvas, not cream paper and not a photographed
whiteboard. Most structure lives in graphite gray and soft black, with a small
semantic color kit: yellow for marker emphasis, blue for system paths, green for
healthy progress, red for risk or rejection, and purple only for occasional
agent or model state. The colors are labels in the argument, never a brand
gradient. Why: the white field makes the explanation feel clean and inspectable,
while disciplined semantic color lets the viewer parse state quickly.

## Typographic Voice
Favor a readable handwritten Chinese-capable voice for titles, callouts, and
short labels - LXGW WenKai or a close WenKai-style face is the canonical feel -
paired with a quiet sans or mono voice for compact technical tokens, file names,
commands, and status tags. Keep text short and diagram-native. Why: the
handwritten face gives the deck the "someone is explaining this live" quality,
but technical identifiers still need mechanical clarity.

## Spatial Temperament
It wants an organized explainer diagram: left-top title, clear card groups,
precise arrows, margins that leave the canvas breathable, and a bottom takeaway
lane that can summarize the current point. The composition can be a route,
split comparison, loop, stack, queue, tower, or decision gate, but the audience
must always know what is active, what changed, and where to look next. The
layout is allowed to be dense; it is not allowed to be sloppy.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface is a flat digital canvas with just enough hand-made character:
lightly imperfect outlines, soft sketch strokes, marker highlights, and
stickerized emoji or small object icons attached to real nodes. Depth is shallow
and functional - a card edge, a lifted label, a stamped result - never glossy
chrome. The atmosphere is clean, bright, and teachable.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring identity marks are engineering explanation artifacts: annotated
cards, precise arrows, state badges, small callout labels, yellow marker swipes,
stamp-like verdicts, and emoji stickers used as semantic objects for roles,
agents, files, tools, or incidents. The bottom takeaway can appear as a final
sentence with a marker highlight over the key phrase. Use emoji only when they
are embedded into the diagram grammar; naked floating emoji reads childish.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Motion is beat-driven teaching. Each beat should reveal a new argument state:
cards slide in like paper, arrows draw along their route, statuses stamp down,
marker highlights sweep left to right, and active nodes pulse or settle with a
gentle spring. Scene transitions should be restrained and directional only when
the new scene has a spatial reason. Why: the animation is the explanation
unfolding, so purely decorative motion makes the system harder to understand.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms

Identity Invariants:
- True white engineering canvas, never warm aged paper or dark board
- Handwritten Chinese-capable title/callout voice paired with precise technical
  sans or mono text
- Organized diagram structure with cards, arrows, labels, and explicit active
  states
- Small semantic color kit, especially yellow marker emphasis
- Stickerized emoji or object icons only when attached to real diagram meaning
- Beat-driven reveal motion that advances the argument
- Bottom takeaway lane or equivalent summary moment for the current point

Freedoms:
- Diagram topology: route, loop, stack, split, queue, gate, or system map
- Exact semantic color assignment when the deck defines it clearly
- Whether a scene is sparse teaching or medium-high density explanation
- Which emoji or object stickers appear
- Scene transition direction and timing when it follows the content metaphor
- How often the bottom takeaway appears, as long as key teaching beats have one

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies when it turns into the wrong board: aged paper, tape, sticky-note walls,
deep chalkboard backgrounds, messy doodles, pure tracker UI, decorative
animation, or emojis dropped in as loose decoration. It also fails when all
content appears at once with no beat logic, because the style depends on the
viewer seeing the explanation unfold. If the deck needs warm collaborative mess,
use Sketch Board Emoji. If it needs dark derivation and formulas, use Blackboard
Chalk Talk. If it needs a real diagnostic tool surface, use Debug Reaction
Board.
