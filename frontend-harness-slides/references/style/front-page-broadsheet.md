# Front-Page Broadsheet

## Aesthetic Thesis
The screen behaves like a folded morning paper spread open on a kitchen table:
dense, ink-heavy, authoritative — a single glance tells you something important
happened and the details are waiting in column after column of set type. The
three-second impression is gravity: this is news, and it demands reading.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Authoritative, serious, unhurried — the register of a trusted daily's front page.
Reach for it on narrative-heavy presentations, long-form story arcs, and
content-rich briefings where a great deal of text must live on one screen and
still feel intentional rather than crowded. It fights airy minimalism, playful
tones, and single-idea hero slides; the density that reads as credible here would
read as suffocating in a keynote moment.

## Color Temperament
Live entirely in a newsprint value world: an off-white ground warmed by the
memory of pulp, set against dense, near-absolute black ink. A single restrained
editorial accent is permitted but quarantined to the nameplate or a kicker — it
must never touch body copy, imagery, or rules. Everything else is grayscale:
halftone photographs, tonal separations, the quiet hum of ink on paper. Why:
the broadsheet's authority rests on its ink-and-paper sobriety; introduce a
second color or let the accent bleed into the body, and it becomes advertising
instead of journalism. Refuse saturated imagery, gradient fills, and any color
that has not been through a halftone press.

## Typographic Voice
The nameplate carries a heavy, architectural display character — letterforms
that feel carved or cast, with enough weight to anchor a full page of dense
type. Body text is a classic text face set justified in narrow columns, the
kind that disappears into reading rather than calling attention to itself.
Bylines and datelines sit in small capitals, quiet and institutional.
Hierarchy is deep and fine-grained: nameplate, headline, deck, byline, drop-cap
lead, body, caption — each step distinct but never shouted. Why: the
broadsheet's power is that it makes dense text feel navigable and trustworthy;
flatten the hierarchy and the page becomes an unbroken wall of words no one
will climb.

## Spatial Temperament
This style wants genuine density: multi-column body text filling most of the
canvas, with the nameplate and headline architecture doing the work of
orientation rather than whitespace. The grid feels like a typesetter's page —
tight gutters, hairline column rules, furniture placed with institutional
precision. The invariant is the relationship: dense justified columns anchored
by a dominant nameplate and bounded by hairline rules. Where the headline sits,
how many columns, and whether a halftone image breaks the flow are all free —
but the page must feel full, never sparse.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface is newsprint: slightly warm, absorbent, flat. Depth comes from ink
density and the weight of set type, not from shadows or elevation. Hairline
rules draw the page's architecture without pretending to be three dimensional.
The atmosphere is serious and slightly hushed — a newsroom at press time, not a
broadcast floor.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring identity marks are editorial furniture: a heavy nameplate across
the top, dateline and byline in small capitals, a drop-cap leading the first
paragraph, hairline rules dividing columns, and a single halftone image that
earns its place by being essential to the story. Use them to structure reading,
never to ornament — they are the front page's grammar. The moment a rule grows
thick or a nameplate becomes decorative, it stops being this style.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement is minimal and print-respectful: a quiet page turn, a gentle fade
between sections, nothing out of place in a well-bound volume. Keep tempo slow
and easing soft; the page should feel settled, not performed. Why: the
broadsheet's authority comes from its stillness and permanence; energetic motion
or flashy transitions would contradict the "record of record" composure the
whole style is built on.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms
Identity Invariants:
- Newsprint off-white ground with dense black ink and a grayscale halftone world
- One restrained editorial accent, confined to nameplate or kicker only
- Dense justified multi-column body text that fills the canvas
- A heavy, architectural nameplate that anchors the page
- Hairline rules and editorial furniture (dateline, byline small caps, drop-cap)
  as structure, never decoration

Freedoms:
- Number and width of columns, headline placement and scale
- Whether a halftone image appears, and where it breaks the column flow
- Nameplate display temperament, which story leads, decks and captions
- Page-turn and section transition specifics

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies when it stops feeling like journalism: adding a second accent color or
letting the accent bleed into body text turns it into Magazine Masthead. Airy
margins and sparse layouts strip it of density and leave it looking like a
parody — a fake-news gag rather than a real front page. Saturated imagery, heavy
shadows, or glossy surfaces read as advertising, not reporting. If the content
is evidence-first research rather than narrative storytelling, switch to
Research Memo instead of forcing broadsheet columns onto data that wants a memo
hierarchy.
