# Interactive Dialogue Stage

## Aesthetic Thesis
A small, dim theater — or a live console — where the drama is a conversation: two
voices, roles, or systems taking turns while the audience watches the exchange
unfold. The point is the back-and-forth itself, so the whole surface should feel
staged for dialogue.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Conversational and quietly dramatic, with a digital, attentive edge. Reach for it
when the story is an exchange — human and agent, client and server, question and
answer, role handoffs, clarifying-before-acting — where showing the turn-taking
teaches more than a bullet list. It fights data-heavy reporting and broad
multi-topic overviews; the moment there is no dialogue to stage, the theater is
empty.

## Color Temperament
Set the stage dark — a deep, cool near-black with a hint of blue — so the
speakers read as lit figures against shadow. Text is crisp and light for
legibility. Reserve one or two living accent colors to mark who is active right
now (a warm human tone versus a cool digital one is a natural split), and keep
everything not currently speaking dimmed toward a muted slate. Why: darkness plus
a single lit speaker is what creates focus and turn-taking legibility; if
everything is equally bright, the audience cannot feel whose turn it is.

## Typographic Voice
Favor a clean, technical, structured character — letterforms that feel like an
interface or a transcript, often with a monospaced or precisely spaced quality —
because the digital, structured feel supports the console-and-exchange metaphor.
Keep speaker labels and turns clearly differentiated in voice or weight so the
eye can follow who is talking. Why: a soft or ornamental voice fights the crisp,
this-is-a-system register the style depends on.

## Spatial Temperament
It reads as an exchange with two clear voices and a sense of sequence — the
composition should make "this, then a response" legible, whether that alternation
runs vertically, as a split, or as some other staged arrangement. Give each turn
room to be read before the next, and let the active turn feel foregrounded. How
the two sides are arranged, whether it is a thread, a split, or a staged scene,
and where any avatars or markers sit are all free; the invariant is legible
two-party turn-taking, not a specific split.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface feels like a dark stage or a glowing console: matte shadow with
softly lit elements, gentle glow around active speakers, and clean edges on any
panel or bubble. Depth comes from light and layering — the active voice lifts
forward, the inactive recedes into the dark. The atmosphere is focused and
slightly cinematic, like a spotlit conversation.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring identity marks are the grammar of conversation: distinct speaker
markers or avatars, turn containers (bubbles, panels, or console blocks), and a
clear active-speaker highlight. Use them to make roles and sequence unmistakable,
not as decoration. The glow or emphasis should always mean "attention is here
now."

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement is turn-based and directed: a new turn arrives and takes focus while the
previous one dims or settles back, attention visibly passing from one voice to
the other. Interaction can let the viewer step through the exchange, expand a
turn, or reveal a system's reasoning. Keep motion purposeful and speaker-to-
speaker rather than ambient. Why: the animation is narrating who speaks next, so
decorative or simultaneous motion blurs the dialogue.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms
Keep constant to stay this style: a dark cool stage, crisp light text, one or two
accent colors reserved for the active speaker, a technical or console typographic
voice, legible two-party turn-taking, and light-based focus on whoever is
speaking. Free to vary on every slide: the arrangement of the two sides, avatar
treatment, how a turn is revealed, the specific accents, and whether the exchange
feels intimate or system-like.

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies when the conversation disappears: a bright flat background, no clear
speakers, everything lit equally, a soft decorative typeface, or a single
monologue with no exchange to stage. If the content is really a report or a broad
overview, do not force it onto a stage — there is no dialogue to watch; use a
reading-first or overview style instead.
