# Kinetic Type Punchline

## Aesthetic Thesis
A loud graphic poster where words are the image: a few massive, stacked phrases
hit the eye like a headline shout, delivering one punch the audience cannot miss.
Type is not labeling the slide — type is the slide.

## Emotional Register & Fit
High-energy, graphic, and confrontational in a fun way — the register of a bold
poster or a mic-drop. Reach for it on section punches, hard contrasts, memorable
one-liners, and moments meant to wake a room up. It fights reading-first detail
and nuance; there is no room for a paragraph, so keep it away from evidence,
explanation, or anything that needs careful qualification.

## Color Temperament
Commit to extreme contrast with a tiny palette — typically a dominant hard field
(a deep black or one high-voltage saturated color) carrying its near-opposite in
type, plus at most one more electric accent for a struck or swapped word. There
are essentially no mid-tones and no gradients; the punch comes from maximum
figure-ground contrast. Why: the impact is a function of contrast and restraint —
introduce a third or fourth color and the shout becomes noise.

## Typographic Voice
This style lives or dies on display type, so use the heaviest, most condensed,
most confident letterforms available — a compressed, ultra-bold, poster-grotesque
energy — usually set tight and often uppercase so the words stack into dense
blocks. Any supporting text is deliberately tiny, a whisper beneath the shout.
Why: the drama is the mass and weight of the letters filling the field; a
normal-weight or airy typeface simply cannot land a punchline.

## Spatial Temperament
It wants type to dominate the field — a few words scaled to fill most of the
surface, stacked and packed so they read as a graphic block, with only a sliver
of space for anything else. Composition can be centered or aggressively
off-balance, but it should feel like a poster, not a slide with a title. Which
words stack, their alignment, and where the tiny support sits are free; the
invariant is that type owns the space and hits instantly.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface is flat, hard, and graphic — no texture, no depth tricks, no soft
light; the power is in pure color fields and letterform edges. Any sense of depth
is graphic layering (a word overlapping a field, a struck word behind a
replacement), not lighting. The atmosphere is charged and immediate, like a
printed protest poster or a title card.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring identity moves are typographic: words stacked into dense columns, a
key word struck through and replaced by a louder one, and phrases broken across
lines for rhythm and emphasis. Use these to carry meaning — a strike-and-replace
literally shows an idea being overturned. Restraint on everything non-type keeps
the gesture sharp.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement is percussive and snappy: words slam in, a struck word gets crossed and
swapped, lines hit one after another with impact rather than drifting. This is
the one style in this family where fast, hard, even jarring motion is correct.
Keep it decisive — a punch, not a fade. Why: the motion is delivering the
punchline's timing, so slow, gentle easing would defuse the hit.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms
Keep constant to stay this style: extreme two-tone contrast with at most one
extra electric accent, the heaviest condensed display voice usually tight and
uppercase, type dominating the field, flat graphic surfaces, and decisive
percussive motion. Free to vary on every slide: which dominant color leads, the
stacking and alignment of words, whether a word is struck and swapped, and the
exact timing of the hits.

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies the moment it gets polite: light or medium type, comfortable whitespace
with small words, soft gradients or textures, several colors, or gentle fades. It
also dies if you try to make it explain — the instant a slide needs real
sentences or data, this is the wrong instrument; move the detail to a
reading-first style and keep this for the punch.
