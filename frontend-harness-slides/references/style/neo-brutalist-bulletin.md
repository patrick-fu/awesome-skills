# Neo-Brutalist Bulletin

## Aesthetic Thesis
The screen behaves like a protest poster freshly taped to a concrete wall:
thick black rules, blunt shadows, one electric accent shouting through a field
of paper. The audience reads it in about three seconds and feels something
between a bulletin and a declaration — unignorable, unapologetic, built to
hold real information without losing its bite.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Confident, blunt, slightly irreverent — the energy of a zine or a protest
handbill that happens to be well-organized. Reach for it on product launches,
process overviews, comparison decks, and any presentation that needs to carry
dense information (stats, tables, step sequences) while still feeling bold and
human. It fights delicate subtlety, reverent minimalism, and anything that
needs to whisper; the moment a slide tries to be precious or ethereal, this
instrument crushes it.

## Color Temperament
Live on a warm off-white paper ground — never pure clinical white, never
cream — set against heavy black structure. One high-voltage accent color is
the system's spark: a saturated, slightly acidic hue that reads as signal
against both paper and ink. Use it sparingly: one accent region per slide, a
word highlight inside a headline, an affirmative pill in a table, a single
chart series. Never two accents. Why: the voltage comes from scarcity; spread
the accent across multiple regions and it stops being a signal and starts
being wallpaper. Refuse soft gradients, background patterns, and any mid-tone
that would muddy the paper-ink-accent triad.

## Typographic Voice
Display type is a heavy grotesque sans set strictly uppercase with tight,
compressed letter-spacing — blunt, loud, slightly mechanical in its
confidence. This is the voice that shouts the headline. Body text drops to a
lighter weight in sentence case, calm and readable, creating a sharp
hierarchical gap between the shout and the explanation. Labels and metadata
use a narrow, tracked-out uppercase treatment that reads as system chrome
rather than editorial. Why: the contrast between heavy uppercase display and
light sentence-case body is what makes dense slides feel organized instead of
shouty; flatten that gap and the whole deck reads as one unmodulated yell.

## Spatial Temperament
This style is density-capable by design. Content fills the canvas from edge
to edge, divided into bordered regions that meet without gaps. The grid is
felt rather than shown — thick black rules carve the space into panels, and
each panel holds its own piece of the argument (a stat, a step, a comparison
column). The invariant is the relationship between border weight and content
density: thicker rules carry denser panels, thinner rules carry lighter
chrome. Exact panel count, span, and arrangement are all free; the system
stays intact as long as the borders are doing the structural work and the
paper ground is visible between them.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface is uncoated paper: flat, matte, slightly warm to the touch.
Depth comes from two sources only — hard offset shadows (solid black, zero
blur, shifted bluntly down and to the right) and the contrast of adjacent
colored panels. There is no soft elevation, no glow, no gradient falloff. The
atmosphere is sharp and slightly rough, like something printed quickly on
heavy stock and nailed up before the ink had time to be polite.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring identity marks are: a thick black border around every
substantive region (the frame is the layout); a hard offset drop-shadow on
elevated cards and callouts (never blurred, never colored); a single
high-voltage accent swatch wrapping a word inside a headline (the system's
emphasis mechanism); and a small uppercase label-pill as section tag or
status chip. Use these with intent — a border carves space, a shadow lifts a
key claim, an accent marks a signal — never as decoration.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement is snappy, blocky, and un-eased. Elements snap into place, slide
in from an edge, or pop on fully formed — no slow drifts, no springy
bounces, no gradual fade-ins. Transitions between slides are instant or
near-instant. Interaction, where it exists, is crisp and mechanical: a tap
produces an immediate state change with a sharp visual feedback. Why: the
motion must match the blunt, no-nonsense character of the borders and the
type; eased, organic movement would contradict the whole bulletin-board
energy and read as indecisive.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms

Identity Invariants:
- A warm off-white paper ground (never pure white, never dark)
- Heavy black borders and rules doing the structural layout work
- Hard offset drop-shadows in solid black with zero blur
- One high-voltage accent color, used sparingly as signal
- Heavy grotesque sans uppercase with tight tracking for all display
- Blocky, un-eased motion and instant-feel transitions

Freedoms:
- Which specific accent hue (any high-saturation, high-voltage color works)
- Exact panel count, arrangement, and span within the bordered grid
- Whether a given slide carries two dense panels or six compact ones
- The specific content type per panel (stat, quote, step, comparison cell)
- The exact border weight within a reasonable "thick" range
- Whether label-pills appear on a given slide

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies when it gets polite: soft gradients replacing the hard accent,
delicate serif type creeping into headlines, timid spacing that leaves large
empty regions, blurred or colored shadows pretending to be elevation, or a
second accent color diluting the signal. It also breaks when forced onto
content that is purely data-for-data's-sake — if every slide is a comparison
matrix and the only job is side-by-side numerical evaluation, this style's
bulletin energy becomes noise. Switch to Benchmark Matrix for pure data
comparison instead of trying to strip this down to a table-only system.
