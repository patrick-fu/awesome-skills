# Object Metaphor Hero

## Aesthetic Thesis
An abstract idea made tangible: preparation, planning, or a toolkit rendered as a
real, well-made physical object or an organized kit, so the audience grasps
something conceptual by seeing it as a thing they could pick up. The object
carries the meaning.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Tactile, prepared, and reassuring — the register of a well-packed kit or a
craftsman's bench. Reach for it when an abstract concept (readiness, context, a
set of capabilities) becomes clearer as a physical metaphor, or when you want
"we brought the right tools" to feel concrete. It fights dense side-by-side
comparison and pure data; if the point is not served by a single tangible
metaphor, the object becomes a gimmick.

## Color Temperament
Ground it in warm, natural, material tones — sand, cream, tan, and the browns,
ambers, and golds of leather, wood, brass, or kraft — so the surface feels like
real, quality materials rather than screen color. Keep the palette earthy and
cohesive, with contrast coming from material warmth and shadow rather than bright
accents. Why: the warmth and restraint are what let the object read as a crafted,
trustworthy thing; cool or neon color turns a tactile metaphor into a UI.

## Typographic Voice
Pair a clean, structured character for labels and callouts with a more classic,
considered voice for titles — the labels feel like neat tags on a kit, the titles
feel authored. Keep type calm and well-set so it supports the object rather than
competing with it. Why: the hero is the object, so loud or quirky type pulls
focus from the metaphor the whole style is built to carry.

## Spatial Temperament
It wants a clear focal object or a sense of organized compartments — either one
hero thing given room and reverence, or a tidy set of parts each in its place,
with supporting ideas relating to the object spatially. The arrangement should
feel deliberate and well-kept, like something packed with care. Whether it is a
single centered hero or a compartmentalized kit, where the parts sit, and how
labels connect are all free; the invariant is that a tangible object or organized
set anchors the meaning.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface should feel physical and lightly textured — grain, weave, paper, or
matte material — with soft, believable shadow giving objects real weight and
presence. Depth comes from gentle real-world lighting and layering, not flat
graphics. The atmosphere is calm, crafted, and a little reverent, like a
considered flat-lay.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring identity marks are object cues: a hero item rendered with care,
organized compartments or slots, neat tags and labels, and connectors that relate
parts to the whole. Use them so each part means something — a tool, a capability,
a step. The sense of order and craft is the signature; a sloppy or arbitrary
arrangement breaks it.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement is assembly-like and gentle: parts settle into their compartments, a
hero object eases in or slowly turns to be seen, pieces come together to form the
whole. Interaction can open a compartment, highlight a part, or reveal what a
tool does. Keep motion unhurried and physical, respecting the object's weight.
Why: the animation is showing something being prepared or assembled, so frantic
or weightless motion contradicts the tactile care.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms
Keep constant to stay this style: warm natural material tones, contrast from
warmth and shadow rather than neon, a calm label voice paired with a considered
title voice, a tangible object or organized kit as the anchor, physical texture
and believable shadow, and gentle assembly-like motion. Free to vary on every
slide: which object or kit carries the idea, single-hero versus compartment
arrangement, the specific materials evoked, and how parts are revealed.

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies when it goes flat and digital: cool screen-blue palettes, neon accents,
hard flat UI cards, shadowless graphics, or a loud typeface stealing focus from
the object. It also dies without a real metaphor — if there is no object that
genuinely clarifies the idea, do not invent a decorative one; use a cleaner
evidence or comparison style instead.
