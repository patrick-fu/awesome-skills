# Retro Windows

## Aesthetic Thesis
The screen behaves like a booted desktop from 1995: every slide is a window
sitting on a gray desktop, complete with beveled chrome, a navy title bar, and
a three-button cluster winking in the corner. The three-second impression is
"this is software I used to click on" — a knowingly nostalgic costume that
lands as instantly recognizable and slightly mischievous.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Playful, winking, and functionally dense — the register of a demo disk or a
shareware application. Reach for it on product launches with a retro hook,
internal tools showcases, nostalgia-themed keynotes, and any deck where the
content benefits from a "this is a working application" framing. It fights
elegant minimalism, premium reverence, and calm authority; the costume is so
specific and loud that it would steamroll a subtle message.

## Color Temperament
Live entirely inside the Win9x system gray family for all chrome and structure:
a slightly warm button-face gray, a darker desktop gray, and white reserved for
content wells and sunken panels. Navy is the sole primary accent — it owns
title bars, progress fills, chart bars, and headline text. A small cluster of
retro status hues (DOS green, brick red, mustard yellow, teal cyan) carries
fixed semantic meaning: green for OK, red for warnings, yellow for moderate,
cyan for tertiary data. These are signals, not paint. Refuse saturated modern
hues, gradients that are not the title-bar navy, and any border that is not a
two-tone bevel. Why: the palette is the era — introduce a modern brand color
and the illusion shatters instantly.

## Typographic Voice
The workhorse is a system-sans character with a pixel-era feel: small, fixed,
and utilitarian, the way software UI looked before fluid type. Headlines inside
a window body speak in navy at a modestly larger scale with bold weight — the
application-title voice, not a keynote voice. Two nostalgic accents appear
sparingly: a chunky pixel-display face for splash-screen punctuation and a CRT
terminal mono for nav hints and terminal regions. Italic and underline are
absent; emphasis comes from weight and color alone. Why: the fixed-pixel,
system-font character is what makes the deck read as "running on a CRT" rather
than "designed in 2026."

## Spatial Temperament
Density is non-negotiable. Every window body should feel packed with stacked
panels, group-boxes, KPI strips, charts, and status footers — the way a real
application screen has multiple regions doing concurrent work. Sparse slides
with one centered headline read as broken or unfinished. The invariant is the
window chrome framing a dense body: the beveled rectangle with its title bar
and button cluster must appear on every slide. How the body is subdivided —
panels, group-boxes, side-by-side windows — is free, but the density ratio is
not.

## Material & Atmosphere
Depth comes entirely from bevel illusions: every surface is either raised
(highlight on top-left, shadow on bottom-right) or sunken (the inverse). There
are no soft drop shadows, no blurred glows, no rounded corners. A faint CRT
scanline overlay sits above everything, giving the whole deck the texture of a
phosphor monitor. The atmosphere is functional and slightly dorky — the
earnestness of a period when software UI was proud to look like software UI.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring identity marks are the window chrome itself: a beveled-raised
rectangle with a navy gradient title bar carrying a left icon-lockup and
filename (always uppercase with extension — METRICS.LOG, README.DOC) and a
right three-button cluster (minimize, maximize, close). Other marks include the
notched group-box title, the chevron-prefixed list, the literal-x checkbox, and
the sunken white panel that signals "this is content, not chrome." Each is a
functional element of the OS illusion, never decoration.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement is blocky and instant: windows snap into place, progress fills jump to
their width, transitions happen without easing. The feel is clicks and snaps,
not glides and drifts. A marquee may scroll text at a steady linear pace as a
period-appropriate flourish. Interaction, if present, is binary — a button
depresses (bevel inverts) or it does not. Why: smooth eased motion belongs to
modern interfaces; the whole costume depends on the jerky, mechanical feel of
1990s software.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms
Identity Invariants:
- OS window chrome on every slide (beveled-raised rectangle + navy title bar +
  three-button cluster)
- Gray system palette with navy as the sole primary accent
- Fixed semantic meaning for the four retro status colors
- System-sans workhorse type with pixel-era character
- Bevel-based depth (raised/sunken), never soft shadows or rounded corners
- Dense, application-UI body composition
- Blocky, uneased motion

Freedoms:
- Window count and arrangement (single, side-by-side, stacked)
- Which filename titles each window and which icon letter it carries
- Body subdivision into panels, group-boxes, charts, and KPI strips
- Whether the pixel-display or terminal accent face appears (and where)
- Content type and data — the costume is content-agnostic

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies when it gets smooth or elegant: rounded corners, soft drop shadows,
modern brand colors, fluid eased motion, an elegant serif display, generous
whitespace, or a single centered headline. It also breaks when the status
colors are redeployed as decoration rather than signals. If the content needs
serious authority, calm credibility, or premium restraint, this costume is too
loud and too specific — switch to Operating Manual for a serious dark runbook
that carries real weight without winking at the audience. If the content wants
arcade game retro with pixel sprites and HUD meters rather than OS desktop
nostalgia, switch to Arcade Boss Fight. If it needs analog cassette-package
warmth with product catalogue feel instead of software UI costume, move to
Cassette-Era Packaging.
