# Sketch Board Emoji

## Aesthetic Thesis
A warm, in-progress workshop whiteboard where thinking is happening out loud:
hand-drawn marks, stuck-on notes, and a small cast of emoji actors make a process
feel human, collaborative, and unfinished in the best way. The audience should
feel invited into the room rather than presented at.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Approachable, playful, and candid — the register of a good facilitator, not a
keynote speaker. Reach for it on collaboration stories, messy-to-structured
journeys, human-in-the-loop workflows, and anywhere warmth lowers the audience's
guard. It fights formal, regulated, or high-gravitas material; the hand-drawn
looseness reads as charming in a workshop and as unserious in a boardroom, so
keep it away from legal, financial, or executive-gravitas decks unless the
irreverence is deliberate.

## Color Temperament
Build on a warm, slightly aged paper base — a cream that feels recycled and
handled rather than bright and new — because the warmth signals informality and
safety. Marks and text sit in a soft natural charcoal rather than hard black, as
if made with a real pen. Accents are a small kit of friendly, slightly muted
highlighter tones (a note yellow, a tape blue, a caution red are typical), each
carrying loose meaning — draft, link, risk — but never neon and never dominant.
Why: saturated, precise color breaks the handmade truce; these hues should look
like supplies on a desk, not a brand palette.

## Typographic Voice
Lead with a genuine handwriting or hand-sketched character for titles and margin
notes — letterforms with human wobble and personality — set against a clean,
quiet everyday voice for anything that must stay easy to read. Let the
handwritten hand carry warmth and the plain hand carry clarity, and never let the
handwriting swell so large it turns illegible. Why: "someone actually wrote this,
and you can still read it comfortably" is the whole promise; all-handwriting goes
unreadable, all-clean goes corporate.

## Spatial Temperament
It wants a lightly scattered, pinned-up feel — elements that look placed by hand,
gently rotated, slightly overlapping, connected by loose lines or arrows — with
enough breathing room to read as organized-messy rather than cluttered. The
arrangement should feel gathered, not gridded, yet nothing should truly collide
or hide behind something else. Which notes go where, how the actors are staged,
and how connections route are all free; the invariant is the informal,
hand-arranged temperament, not any fixed placement.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface is tactile and papery, quietly textured like a real board or sheet,
sometimes with faint grid or grain to reinforce that this is a physical surface.
Depth comes from small real-world cues — a note's slight lift, a strip of tape, a
soft shadow under a sticky — rather than from digital gloss. The atmosphere is
busy-but-friendly, like walking up to a board mid-session.

## Signature Gestures
The recurring identity marks are the artifacts of hands at work: sticky notes,
bits of tape, hand-drawn borders and connectors, and a small cast of emoji or
simply drawn actors standing in for people, systems, or roles. Use them as
meaningful characters and links, not confetti — each note or actor should carry a
real idea. Their slight imperfection is the point; perfectly aligned, they stop
feeling handmade.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement should feel like drawing and placing in real time — lines that sketch
themselves in, notes that get stuck on with a little settle, actors that pop or
nudge to take a turn. Interaction is warm and tactile: a note lifts on hover, a
connector traces on focus, an actor reacts. Keep the tempo light and slightly
imperfect rather than mechanically smooth. Why: the motion should echo a person
working the board, so overly precise, springy-perfect animation contradicts the
handmade feel.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms

Identity Invariants:
- Warm aged-paper base (cream that feels recycled and handled)
- Soft charcoal hand-marks (never hard black)
- Small friendly non-neon accent kit (yellow, blue, red are typical)
- Real handwritten voice paired with a readable plain voice
- Hand-arranged looseness — gathered, not gridded
- Tactile paper/tape/note materials

Freedoms:
- Which actors appear
- How notes and connectors are arranged
- Specific hand-drawn shapes
- Exact motion and timing
- How much structure versus mess a given beat carries

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies with hard precision: crisp geometric grids, pure black on pure white,
saturated brand-neon accents, a purely typeset look with no hand marks, or slick
perfectly-eased motion. If the content needs gravitas, dense tabular data, or
formal authority, do not dress it in sticky notes — the warmth undercuts the
seriousness; move it to a calmer, more formal style instead. If the content
needs warm hands-on structure with kitchen-prep tangibility rather than loose
workshop energy, switch to Kitchen Prep Station. If it wants friendly
approachable polish with pastel softness instead of hand-drawn mess, move to
Soft Pastel Friendly.
