# Widescreen Title Card

## Aesthetic Thesis
The screen behaves like a cinema curtain lifting: a deep letterbox frame opens
onto a graded film still, and a title hangs in the dark with the gravity of a
film's opening credit. The audience should feel the hush of a theater going
dark — they get it in about three seconds and lean forward.

## Emotional Register & Fit
Immersive, atmospheric, slightly reverent — the mood of a film's opening
moment rather than a deck's first slide. Reach for it on section title cards,
keynote openings, and chapter transitions where a single name or idea must land
with cinematic weight before the real content begins. It fights dense
information, multi-part layouts, and anything that needs more than a title
plus a whisper of context; the moment a slide has to explain itself in
paragraphs, this instrument is the wrong one.

## Color Temperament
Live in a deep, warm-black world — not pure monitor black but a filmic black
with a hint of warmth, the way projected light hits a dark theater. A graded
photographic or painted still carries the color temperature of the scene; it
is the ground, not a decoration. Accent color is nearly absent — perhaps a
single warm highlight drawn from the still itself, used as a scarce credit or
subtle marker. Why: the atmosphere comes from the still and the dark together;
introducing unrelated color pulls the viewer out of the cinema and into a
presentation. Refuse flat UI chrome, bright fills, and any color that does not
belong to the still's own palette.

## Typographic Voice
The title carries film-poster character — letterforms with presence and
drama, whether that drama comes from bold condensed authority or from elegant,
high-contrast elegance. It sits large in the letterbox frame, unhurried,
allowed to breathe. Supporting text — a subtitle, a credit, a date — stacks
quietly beneath or beside it, small and recessive, the cast list beneath the
poster's name. Why: the title is the whole reason this card exists; giving
secondary text equal weight turns a title card into a content slide, and the
cinematic spell breaks.

## Spatial Temperament
The letterbox is the law: a wide cinematic aspect with deep black bars above
and below, compressing the visible stage into a horizontal band that feels
like a screen, not a page. Within that band, the graded still fills the
ground and the title floats in its own calm zone. The invariant is the
letterbox ratio and the still-as-ground; where the title sits within the frame
— centered, left-anchored, drifting low — is free, so long as it reads as a
film credit, not a bullet point.

## Material & Atmosphere
The surface is projected light on a dark theater wall: the still carries
texture and atmosphere, the bars are pure void, and the title sits between
them as if lit from the same source. Depth comes from the graded image's own
perspective and from the title's subtle separation from it — a faint glow or
soft shadow that says "this is in front of the film." The atmosphere is
expectant, immersive, slightly hushed.

## Signature Gestures
The letterbox bars are the signature — the instant you see that wide framed
band, you know you are in a title card, not a regular slide. The graded still
as ground is the second mark: not a gradient mesh, but a real or painted
scene, graded for mood. The film-poster title treatment is the third. Used
together they say "opening credit" without needing a word of explanation.

## Motion & Interaction Register
Movement is slow and cinematic: a gentle fade-in of the still, the title
settling into place as if the projector has reached full brightness, perhaps a
subtle drift or a slow Ken Burns-like push on the still. Nothing fast,
nothing bouncy, nothing that says "interface." Why: the motion must feel like
cinema, not like a slide transition; a snappy entrance or a UI-style wipe
would yank the audience out of the theater and back into a meeting room.

## Identity Invariants vs Freedoms
Identity Invariants:
- Deep warm-black letterbox bars framing a wide cinematic aspect
- A graded photographic or painted still as the ground layer
- A film-poster title treatment with real dramatic presence
- Supporting text kept recessive and credit-like
- Cinematic atmosphere — the whole card must read as "opening credit," not
  "content slide"

Freedoms:
- Which still or scene carries the ground (match it to the section's mood)
- Title placement within the letterbox frame
- Whether the title voice is bold-condensed or elegant-high-contrast
- Subtitle and credit stacking and exact wording
- Whether the still drifts, pushes in, or holds still
- Any single accent drawn from the still's own palette

## What Breaks the Spell
It dies when the card gets crowded: more than a title plus a line or two of
support, dense paragraphs, bullet lists, data tables, multiple competing
elements, flat UI buttons or chrome, bright unrelated accent colors, or fast
bouncy transitions. If a slide needs to carry real content rather than just
name a section, this style cannot hold it — do not shrink the type or strip
the still to force more words in. Switch to Spotlight Quote Poster for a
dark single-statement slide that can hold a bit more text while keeping the
cinematic mood. If the content calls for light extreme-value keynote restraint
with one idea in a field of white rather than cinematic dark letterbox, switch
to Minimal Product Keynote.
